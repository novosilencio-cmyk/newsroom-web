Level 1 · factual writing

Write vividly without inventing

This course helps you make factual writing feel present and alive while keeping every important sentence within the limits of the available evidence.

The central problem is easy to recognise. A text can be accurate but so abstract that the reader never enters it. The same text can become more vivid by quietly adding an atmosphere, motive, scene or degree of certainty that the sources do not support. The course works between those two failures.

Originator & idea-maker: Mr.BM.Benlolo · Human adaptation and editorial development: Experimental Newsroom human–AI collaboration · Published: 2 September 2026

You begin with a source set that has clear boundaries.

You may use a small collection of documents, interviews, observations or data, as long as you know what belongs to the set and what does not.

You create five pieces of work that can be examined.

You produce a 250–450 word text, a truth map, a five-line movement, a negative control and a short revision note.

You should allow about 100 minutes for the course.

You can complete the work alone. A separate reader can then give feedback without first being told what you intended the text to do.

You can inspect the course before registering

The seven parts lead from source material to a tested factual text.

The preview below describes the purpose and working action in every part. The full course supplies the complete instructions, constructed example, working tables, final assignment and self-assessment.

01

You find an anchor that the sources can carry.

You choose a documented detail, precise fact or genuinely open question that gives the reader somewhere to stand. You also identify the trace that supports it, so vividness begins in evidence rather than decoration.

02

You build movement without manufacturing suspense.

You test a five-part movement from anchor through tension, development and turn to return. The turn must arise from a limitation, contrast or new fact in the material, and the return must deepen the opening rather than merely repeat it.

03

You give every important claim a visible status.

You distinguish what is documented or observed from what a source claims, what you infer, what has been openly reconstructed and what remains unknown. This truth map lets you vary the language without making unlike forms of knowledge sound equally certain.

04

You move deliberately between detail and explanation.

Concrete detail helps the reader experience the subject, while explanation shows how the pieces connect. You inspect each paragraph to see whether it provides presence, understanding or both, and whether its source is strong enough for the work it performs.

05

You compare a thin version, an invented version and a controlled version.

A constructed library case shows how the same source set can produce writing that is defensible but lifeless, vivid but unsupported, or both readable and truthful. The comparison makes the boundary visible in sentences rather than leaving it as an abstract rule.

06

You revise for truth, movement and human consequence in separate passes.

The first pass checks actors, dates, numbers, quotations, causes and degrees of certainty. The second checks how the text moves. The third replaces empty praise or alarm with precise verbs, consequences and respect for human agency.

07

You complete an assignment that another reader can actually test.

You freeze the text before explaining your intention. A reader then marks what is clear, what lacks support, where movement stops, which open question is valuable and which revision can be tested.

TRY

You can try this exercise without registering

Write a five-line movement and test every line against its source.

Choose a small factual subject for which you have at least one reliable source. Do not begin by trying to sound impressive. Begin by deciding what the material can genuinely support.

  1. Write the anchor. Give the reader a concrete, documented starting point.
  2. Name the tension. State what is unresolved, difficult or changing without predicting the outcome.
  3. Develop the picture. Add a source, fact or explanation that makes the issue more intelligible.
  4. Introduce the turn. Use a real limitation, contrast or fact that changes how the first line should be understood.
  5. Return to the opening. Show what the reader can now see that was not visible in the first line alone.

You finish by checking the boundary.

Point to the support for each of the five lines. If the sources cannot carry a complete movement, keep the missing part visibly incomplete. An honest gap is a stronger result than a fluent invention.

The method produces work that can be challenged

You practise, attempt to disprove your own draft and invite an independent reading.

The course does not treat understanding as a feeling. You produce observable work, write one deliberately polished version that the sources do not justify, and mark the exact point where it crosses the line. Another reader then examines the frozen text without first hearing your intention.

Artificial intelligence may help you explore structures, contrast versions, formulate questions and revise language. A human remains responsible for selecting sources, verifying material claims, assessing privacy and harm, deciding whether to publish and correcting errors.
