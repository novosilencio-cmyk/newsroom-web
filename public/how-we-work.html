How we work

Evidence before certainty · constructive without becoming promotional

Our editorial direction

What helps a society keep working?

A public system can look strong on paper and still be hard to use. A small local practice can look modest and still solve something important. We follow the distance between the two: what people, communities, businesses and institutions actually manage to do, what it costs, who carries the work, and whether the result lasts.

For us, societal capacity means the ability to support good lives, secure families, working communities, capable institutions and a natural foundation that can endure. We are value-bound, not party-bound.

What we look for

Start with what changes

A public promise eventually has to meet something concrete: a childcare place, a field, a municipal budget or a family trying to use a service. Did anything actually change? Who had to make it happen, who benefited, who carried the cost, and what became harder, failed or remained unresolved?

The answer may come from a public office, a business, a family, a voluntary group or a local community. We care less about the label than about whether the change works and lasts.

What constructive means

A promising idea gets harder questions

Constructive journalism is not praise with footnotes. If a proposal looks promising, the questions become more demanding: how does it work when somebody has to use it, what evidence supports it, what does it cost, who is left outside, and what would make us change our mind?

A response can be worth examining before we know whether it succeeds. The uncertainty is part of the story.

How we distinguish claims

A decision is not the same as a result

A budget can show that money was allocated. A law can show that a rule changed. A programme page can show what an institution intends to do. None of those facts, by themselves, tell us whether a family received care, a wetland held water, a worker’s day improved or a public service became easier to use.

We separate what we observed, what a source documents or claims, what we infer, and what remains unknown.

People and places

The people doing the work are part of the evidence

Policies do not implement themselves. Someone has to answer the application, negotiate with a landowner, care for a child, repair the system, find the missing money or live with the consequence when a plan does not fit the place.

People affected by a response are not scenery for a thesis. When we have not spoken to them, we say so. A document cannot substitute for their experience.

Writing and form

Let the reader enter the material without inventing the room

Serious reporting does not have to sound like minutes from a meeting. We use sequence, rhythm, reconstruction, metaphor and artistic form when they make documented material easier to understand.

But vividness creates an obligation. We do not invent a person, quotation, feeling, cause or witnessed detail to make a story move. The aim is to bring the reader closer to the object — not closer to our certainty.

Time and uncertainty

Some stories are worth following before the ending exists

A law may be passed in a day while its effects take years. A wetland needs seasons. A family may be certified for a service before a usable appointment appears. A financial commitment can be made before the electricity, customers or revenue arrive.

We may follow events or discovery, but we do not manufacture suspense. Waiting, silence, ecological time and missing evidence can remain visible when they are part of reality.

A useful test

Six questions we return to

  1. What is actually happening?
  2. How do we know?
  3. How does the response work in practice?
  4. Who benefits, and who may be left out or harmed?
  5. What does it require in time, money, competence and natural resources?
  6. What would we need to observe next before drawing a stronger conclusion?

The experiment

Humans and AI can work together. Responsibility cannot disappear between them.

Experimental Newsroom is both a newsroom and an artistic experiment. AI can help us search, compare, organise, question and develop forms; humans can do those things too. We identify AI involvement when it is material to published work, while sources, uncertainty, possible harm, editorial choices and corrections remain part of an explicit responsibility chain. Artistic collaboration can open a story; it cannot turn imagined detail into documented fact.
