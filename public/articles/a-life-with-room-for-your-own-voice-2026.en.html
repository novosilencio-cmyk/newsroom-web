A Life with Room for Your Own Voice

Some of what we call finding ourselves may begin with listening for what we miss when no one is asking anything of us.

Origin and editorial direction: Bjørn Moe Aldema. Writing: Experimental Newsroom, a human–AI collaboration with Sjeherasad as the AI writing role.

25 October 2026

Imagine coming home one evening and closing the door. There is nothing else you have to get done. No one is waiting for an answer, and for a while you do not need to have one.

Perhaps you sit down before turning on the light. Outside, the evening continues. Inside, you gradually sense which kind of silence you have come home to: the one you have longed for, or the one you had hoped to escape.

A person finally resting from other people's expectations and a person who would have liked someone to ask how their day had been may sit in separate rooms and look equally lonely. Through the window, we see someone sitting alone. What the evening means, we do not yet know.

The Life We Have Been Given Words For

Long before we can ask what kind of life we want, others have given us words for a good life. We learn what to strive for, what is best kept hidden, and roughly when the big things should have happened. Around a dinner table, an entire life course can lie between questions about work, love and plans for the future.

Much of this comes from people who wish us well. They pass on what has supported them, perhaps hoping to spare us something they themselves have had to bear. This inheritance can hold both wisdom and narrow limits. It takes time to tell them apart, especially when an expectation has been with us so long that it feels like our own.

Sometimes it begins with an unease we can barely grasp. Everything looks reasonable. We do what is expected, receive the approval and move on. Yet something in us is left behind: a wish we keep putting off, a way of working that never gets the quiet it needs, a longing for people with whom we can be more open.

Then an evening without demands can become a rare place to begin. What have I been missing while trying to make this life work?

The Tree the Carpenter Could Not Use

In the Zhuangzi, the carpenter Shi encounters a great tree he finds unsuitable for timber. Later, the tree appears to him in a dream. The very thing that makes it of little use to the carpenter has allowed it to grow and live. [3]

The carpenter has been looking for materials. The tree answers with its own life.

Suddenly, it becomes harder to accept the judgment without examining it. Has the tree failed, or has the carpenter simply arrived with a purpose the tree does not fit?

The question can follow us home, even though a human life makes different demands from those of a tree. When we call someone difficult to place, what place have we prepared for them? Perhaps they need help finding their footing. Perhaps the expectations need to change. Both possibilities need to remain open long enough for us to discover more than the deviation.

It is also worth asking what happens when we defend time alone. If we say only that it makes us more productive afterwards, we have kept the carpenter's measure. Even rest then acquires its value through what it can later deliver.

A life must have room for hours that do not have to pay off.

Room to Listen

Not everyone has an evening to set aside or a room with a door they can close. Work, caring responsibilities and money limit how much of our time we can call our own. Being able to withdraw is also a question of the conditions life affords us.

When time alone is available and wanted, something may have time to emerge. At first, perhaps only tiredness. Gradually, a wish that has not demanded much attention, but has not gone away either.

Do I want to go on like this?

The answer need not come that evening. It may be enough to let the question stay without immediately filling the room with a new plan. Even trying to find ourselves can become another task to perform well, with demands for progress and a clear result. We can give ourselves a little time before deciding who we now have to become.

But silence need not mean that something is becoming clear. Sometimes it is good because we are finally left in peace. At other times, we need help finding our way out of it. From outside, the difference can be hard to see.

The One Who Could Not Explain

In Hans Christian Andersen's “The Wild Swans”, Elisa must make shirts from nettles to free her brothers from an enchantment. While she works, she must remain silent. Others do not know the reason for her silence and come to suspect her of witchcraft. [4]

We know why Elisa is silent. The people around her do not, and her silence becomes a space they fill with suspicion.

As readers, we have an advantage we seldom have outside the fairy tale. We know the reason. When someone close to us is silent, we have to live for a while with not knowing. Perhaps that is where listening begins: before we have found the explanation.

Elisa's silence costs her dearly. She cannot break it without putting her brothers in danger. It is bound to a condition others do not know, and differs from the wanted time alone with which we began. That difference matters. We can admire endurance in a fairy tale while wishing that people around us did not have to carry pain without being able to speak.

When someone is silent or often alone, we can therefore ask gently and accept that an answer may take time. Giving someone a chance to speak helps little if we have already decided what their words must mean.

Becoming Your Own Friend

Alone, we can also repeat an old explanation, now in our own voice. Perhaps it is so familiar that we no longer hear how harsh it is. Perhaps it diminishes us more than it needs to, or lets us evade a question we ought to stay with.

The Bhagavad Gita confronts us with this dual possibility. In verses 6.5–6.6, the mind can be both friend and adversary. The text connects this to self-mastery and spiritual practice. [5]

Not everything that comes from within, then, is necessarily a good guide. Listening to ourselves can also mean examining what we hear. Is this a conviction I want to stand by, or an old judgment I am still directing at myself?

Someone we trust may help us discover the difference. So may small experiments in life: pausing to notice what we feel before saying yes, saying no while our voice is still uncertain, or staying in a conversation from which we would once have withdrawn. We get to discover what holds when a wish meets a real situation.

And then it also meets other people's needs.

The tree has made us question the carpenter's measure. But how far do we get if we simply reject the demands others place on us? We also live with people who need us. Some obligations we wish to be free of; others we would miss if they disappeared.

In Bhagavad Gita 18.47, one's own duty, even imperfectly performed, is placed above another's duty performed well. This may resonate with someone who has tried to live another person's life. But svadharma, one's own duty, belongs here to a religious and social order; chapter 18 also discusses duties associated with the four varnas, the traditional social categories. It is more demanding than modern advice to follow one's desires. [6]

Here, the stories are allowed to stand in tension with each other. Zhuangzi's tree challenges the demand for usefulness. The Bhagavad Gita reminds us, at the same time, that life also holds responsibility and action. Between these perspectives, we can ask which obligations we ourselves wish to bear, and which we have merely inherited from others.

The question can accompany us out of the room and into life. We do not have to have settled everything to begin answering more honestly.

Outside the Closed Door

It is easier to find words for what we want when someone can bear to hear them. The room we have for our own voice therefore also depends on how we are received.

Do we let a friend decline without ceasing to invite them? Can we take pleasure in a life we would not have chosen ourselves? Can someone who has been away a long time return without first having to defend their absence?

In a Japanese myth, Amaterasu withdraws into a rock cave after Susanoo's rampage. The other deities gather outside. Ame no Uzume's dance and the laughter around it awaken her curiosity and help draw her out again. [7]

There is something to turn towards. But after Amaterasu emerges, the cave is sealed so that she cannot go back inside.

What kind of invitation is it if the way back is closed?

The question follows us out of the myth. Someone who has accepted company one evening has not thereby surrendered the right to want quiet the next. And someone who has needed distance may still wish to be remembered.

A community can keep a place open without requiring it to be filled at once. It can let a no stand today without making it a verdict on all future closeness.

The Evening Again

You have been sitting for a while. Perhaps you have simply rested. Perhaps you have discovered something you want to change. You can stay a little longer, or call someone you would like to speak to.

For someone who wishes they had a person to call, the desire for contact remains unanswered. No beautiful thought about time alone can take the place of a person who is actually there.

From outside, the evenings still look alike. It is only when the person living in that silence gets to speak that the difference can become clear. One wants company. Another feels content alone for a while. Perhaps they will answer differently on another evening.

We can listen without demanding that the answer last for ever.

In Depth: What the Research Says

In 2008, Chua and Koestner reported an association between voluntary, self-determined time alone, lower reported loneliness and greater well-being. The finding concerns the motivation for being alone, not whether it is generally better to live alone. [1]

Across four studies, Nguyen, Ryan and Deci found that solitude could reduce high-arousal feelings, both positive and negative. In the fourth study, freely chosen solitude could lead to relaxation and reduced stress. [2]

These studies do not provide grounds for claiming that people who prefer time alone are wiser, more creative or more evolved than others. Nor do they determine what time alone means for a particular person. The essay's reflections on life choices and belonging extend beyond these limited research findings.

About the Essay and Its Sources

This essay was developed through Experimental Newsroom's human–AI collaboration, following Bjørn Moe Aldema's thematic contribution, with Sjeherasad as the AI writing role. The opening is an imagined situation, not a documented event. Fairy tales, philosophical stories and religious texts are used as distinct conversation partners, not as psychological evidence or expressions of a single shared teaching. The interpretations and retellings are the newsroom's. No interviews were conducted. The research basis is limited and does not constitute a systematic review.

[1] Chua and Koestner (2008).

[2] Nguyen, Ryan and Deci (2018; published online in 2017).

[3] Zhuangzi, “In the Human World”, in China Heritage's selection of translations.

[4] Hans Christian Andersen, “The Wild Swans”, Danish text.

[5] Bhagavad Gita 6.5 and 6.6, in A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada's translation and commentary. Here, ātmā is rendered as “mind”; other translations use “self”. A comparison was made with Swami Mukundananda's edition.

[6] Bhagavad Gita 18.41 and 18.47, in the same edition. Its religious interpretation and social context are not identical to the essay's contemporary questions about chosen and inherited obligations.

[7] Kokugakuin University, Encyclopedia of Shinto: Amaterasu and Amenouzume.
