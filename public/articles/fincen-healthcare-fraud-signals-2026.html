What can a bank know about whether care happened?

FinCEN analysed reports covering $17.5 billion in suspicious activity potentially linked to healthcare fraud. The figure describes financial signals—not proven fraud, verified losses or recovered money.

Why this matters

  • FinCEN analysed 5,702 Bank Secrecy Act reports filed by 471 financial institutions between 1 March 2025 and 28 February 2026.
  • The reports identified approximately $17.5 billion in suspicious activity, including completed and attempted transactions. The total may contain duplication, lawful activity and filer errors.
  • Banks can see money move, but usually cannot identify the healthcare billing method behind it. Investigation must connect financial signals with claims, records and evidence of care.

This document-led report was checked on 10 September 2026 against FinCEN and U.S. Treasury material. No patients, providers, financial institutions or public authorities were interviewed, and the newsroom did not report from the locations described. The opening is an editorial comparison, not witness observation.

A bank statement can show where money went.

It can show an insurance payment entering an account, a transfer leaving soon afterwards, a sudden cash withdrawal or business funds paying an apparently personal bill.

It cannot show whether a nurse entered a room.

Between the payment and the patient lies the central problem in a new federal analysis: financial institutions can see the movement of healthcare money, but usually not the care—or absence of care—that produced the claim.

A large number with strict limits

On 9 September 2026, the U.S. Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, or FinCEN, released an analysis of 5,702 Bank Secrecy Act reports filed by 471 financial institutions during the year ending 28 February 2026.

The reports identified approximately $17.5 billion in suspicious activity potentially connected to healthcare fraud. The figure includes completed and attempted transactions. It is not a finding that $17.5 billion was stolen, lost or recovered.

FinCEN warns that suspicious-activity totals may include money entering and leaving the same account, transfers between accounts, continuing reports, amendments and multiple filings involving the same subject. A report may combine lawful and potentially unlawful activity. Filers can make errors.

The $17.5 billion should therefore not be imagined as one pool of unique illicit proceeds.

What is documented—and what is not

Documented
FinCEN analysed 5,702 reports that identified about $17.5 billion in suspicious activity.
Documented
Depository institutions filed 5,080 reports and accounted for about $15.2 billion of the reported amounts.
Documented limit
FinCEN says suspicious-activity reporting is not a complete picture and may be overly inclusive or cumulative.
Editorial interpretation
The analysis is best read as a map of what financial institutions can see, not a measurement of proven healthcare fraud.
Not established
The report does not say how many flagged subjects were cleared, charged or convicted, or how much unique money was lost, prevented or recovered.

Depository institutions submitted approximately 89 percent of the dataset and accounted for nearly 87 percent of the reported amounts. The two largest banks in the dataset by assets produced 29 percent of all reports; one filed approximately 1,100.

That concentration may reflect customer bases, monitoring systems, reporting practices or several factors at once. It does not establish that 29 percent of the suspected conduct occurred at those institutions.

The average amount per filing was approximately $3.3 million, while the median was about $600,000. The gap shows that larger reports pull the average upwards. It does not reveal how much of that difference comes from large suspected schemes, repeated transactions or aggregation.

A denominator changes the story

Fifty-nine financial cooperatives in Puerto Rico filed 1,641 reports—also approximately 29 percent of the dataset. Most concerned suspected manipulation of financial information used to determine Medicaid eligibility. FinCEN says nearly all described customers reportedly lowering account balances before eligibility meetings, obtaining balance-certification letters and redepositing the money later that day or within a few days. Approximately 67 percent involved reported suspicious activity below $15,000.

This is a specific reporting pattern involving benefit eligibility. It is not evidence that Puerto Rico or cooperative customers have a higher general fraud rate.

Home-health businesses illustrate why the subset matters. Treasury’s public announcement says they were the suspected provider in 20 percent of all reports. The underlying analysis says they appeared in more than 21 percent, rising to nearly 32 percent when the Puerto Rico eligibility reports are removed.

The 32-percent figure does not describe an increase. It describes the same home-health reports after a large, analytically different group is removed from the denominator.

The payment is not the billing event

FinCEN says financial institutions can observe money arriving from government health agencies or private insurers, but in most cases cannot identify whether the underlying conduct involved phantom billing, double billing, upcoding, unnecessary treatment or another method. Detailed descriptions usually appeared only after a customer had been publicly charged or indicted, or after investigators informed the institution about an existing case.

The signal can precede proof. Detailed understanding often travels in the opposite direction—from investigators back to the bank.

The reporting threshold reflects that preliminary role. FinCEN’s 30 March 2026 advisory says institutions must file a Suspicious Activity Report when they know, suspect or have reason to suspect that a transaction involves illegal funds, disguises such funds, evades Bank Secrecy Act rules, lacks an apparent lawful purpose or uses the institution to facilitate crime.

Suspicion can require a report. It is not a conviction.

Institutions must retain the report and supporting documentation for five years and provide it when properly requested by FinCEN, law enforcement or a supervisory agency. That preserves evidence for investigation; it does not turn the original suspicion into adjudicated fact.

The counter-question

For whom might this work?

Investigators may use the reports to find relationships across providers, accounts and jurisdictions. Health-programme administrators may improve payment controls. Banks may refine monitoring around combinations of indicators rather than a single transaction. Patients and taxpayers could benefit if early signals produce well-founded investigations without delaying legitimate care.

What could it cost?

Monitoring requires compliance staff, data systems and investigative capacity. The work does not end when a report is filed: someone must test the financial signal against billing records, programme rules and evidence of actual care.

Blunt application has another possible cost. Legitimate providers—particularly small businesses with unconventional structures—may face heightened scrutiny because their accounts resemble a published pattern. Beneficiaries may encounter suspicion or delay when ordinary financial behaviour resembles eligibility manipulation. FinCEN’s analysis does not measure these possible false-positive or access effects.

Who may be left out?

Financial data provides little direct visibility into patients who did or did not receive care, workers whose services may have been billed inaccurately, or legitimate providers operating in sectors described as vulnerable. Reporting intensity can also be mistaken for prevalence: larger institutions or institutions with stronger monitoring may file more reports, while comparable conduct remains less visible elsewhere.

What evidence would change the conclusion?

A stronger evaluation would connect reports, under appropriate confidentiality safeguards, to unique transactions and subjects; claims records and evidence that services were or were not delivered; investigations, charges, adjudications and exonerations; confirmed losses and recoveries; false-positive rates; differences in reporting practice; and effects on providers and beneficiary access.

A high rate of independent substantiation, earlier intervention and measurable recovery without excluding legitimate providers would strengthen the case for financial monitoring. Low substantiation, substantial duplication or widespread defensive account closures would weaken it.

What remains unknown

The published analysis does not establish how much of the $17.5 billion represents unique money. It does not report how many flagged subjects were investigated, cleared, charged or convicted. Nor does it state how much suspicious activity produced an actual programme loss, how much money was prevented from leaving or how much was recovered.

Because the dataset closes on 28 February 2026, it cannot evaluate the effect of FinCEN’s advisory issued one month later.

Bank records alone cannot show whether a particular nurse entered a room, whether treatment was necessary or whether a claim accurately described the care given. That is not a failure of the bank statement. It is a limit of what the statement is.

The $17.5 billion matters because it marks activity that financial institutions believed deserved attention. Its meaning begins—not ends—with suspicion.

Between signal and proof lies the work that determines whether a pattern is a crime, an error or an incomplete view of legitimate care.
