From farm contracts to school meals: following the money in Cariacica

School-food records in Cariacica, Brazil, let us follow purchases from family-farm associations through invoices and payments. They reveal part of the journey from farm to school, and point to the people whose experience still needs to be heard.

Why this matters

  • Cariacica’s Contract 042/2026 with the rural producers’ association APRODER is worth about R$1.24 million (roughly €210,000) and covers a long list of foods for municipal school meals.
  • Municipal records show money moving beyond the signature: one APRODER budget line was fully liquidated and paid at R$277,817.65 (about €47,100), while APROVISTA’s line reached R$196,072.26 fully paid after a further payment on 4 September.
  • The same public call also contains lines at earlier stages. One CAFC ES budget line showed R$35,815.58 (about €6,100) committed but nothing yet liquidated or paid in the 3 September snapshot. The record does not explain why.

What Cariacica agreed to buy

Mozzarella, lettuce, carrots and cassava appear in a school-food contract signed in Cariacica in March 2026.

The list connects two everyday needs: schools need food, and farms need buyers. Making that connection last requires deliveries, invoices, payment and meals children can eat. Cariacica's public records allow several of those steps to be followed.

The records are unusually useful because they extend beyond the contract itself. A reader can examine amounts committed, claims recognised and money paid to named organisations.

Contract 042/2026 was signed on 3 March with APRODER, the Associação dos Produtores Rurais de Roda D'Água e Região, for R$1,238,598.55, about €210,000. Alongside mozzarella, lettuce, carrots and cassava, the list includes plantain, beetroot, scallions, chayote, kale, cauliflower, yam, oranges, fruit pulp, yoghurt, butter, cornmeal, organic bananas and zucchini. Plantain is a starchy cooking banana; cassava is a starchy root.

Chayote is a pale-green relative of squash and melon, often shaped like a pear. Usually cooked as a vegetable, it has crisp flesh and a mild, faintly sweet flavour. The contract's detail gives the purchasing programme a more tangible form than its total value alone.

That total is spread across several budget commitments. Different school categories or funding lines can appear under the same contract. To avoid counting the same obligation twice, the records need to be followed line by line.

Follow the money further than the signature

Three Portuguese accounting terms help explain the trail. An empenho reserves money for an obligation. A liquidação recognises an amount due after the claim and documents have been checked. Payment records the transfer that follows.

On 3 September, one APRODER line showed R$277,817.65, about €47,100, committed, liquidated and paid in full. A separate record under the same school-food call shows a payment of R$102,285.10, about €17,400, on 31 August. Its linked accounting record identifies invoice NF 000.000.606.

Contract 041/2026 concerns APROVISTA, the Associação de Produtores Rurais de Boa Vista. In the 3 September snapshot, one line had R$196,072.26, about €33,300, committed and R$182,981.08, about €31,000, liquidated and paid. Payment followed on 13 July, three days after the accounting check citing invoice 000.000.199.

Update, 13 September: another payment completes one line

The register checked for the 13 September update records a further R$13,091.18 paid on 4 September as payment 0964/2026. Added to the July payment, it completes the R$196,072.26 committed under line 0955/2026. That line is fully liquidated and paid; it is not a total for all APROVISTA business or the whole programme. APROVISTA payment register

The remaining balance on this line has therefore been paid. How the association distributed the money and what schools received still require other evidence. Comparisons below retain the original 3 September date.

What the public record can show

  1. Contract. A named association has a legal agreement to supply food.
  2. Budget commitment. The municipality has assigned money to that obligation.
  3. Liquidation. The claim has passed an accounting check and an amount due has been recognised.
  4. Payment. Municipal money has actually been transferred to the named organisation.
  5. Still open. The public ledger does not by itself tell us what each member farmer earned, how costly delivery was, how the food performed in school kitchens or what changed for children.

The trail establishes that these purchases moved beyond a signed agreement. Named organisations received municipal money.

What that payment meant for farmers and children lies further along the story.

Supplier records show different stages

The portal also preserves differences between suppliers. At the same point in time, their records can show different stages of work.

On 3 September, most of the APROVISTA line had been paid. ASCOPRUM, the Associação Comunitária de Produtores Rurais de Munguba, had R$77,479.13, about €13,100, committed on one line, of which R$58,069.46, about €9,900, was liquidated and paid. A CAFC ES cooperative line showed R$35,815.58, about €6,100, committed, with nothing yet liquidated or paid.

The CAFC ES entry does not explain the timing. Delivery schedules, documents or other circumstances could account for its position. The evidence supports a difference in recorded stage, without establishing a failure by the supplier.

What reaches the farmer after the association is paid?

After payment reaches an association, the public ledger tells us less.

Brazil's education-development fund, FNDE, says acquisition prices should reflect local supply conditions and the costs required to deliver the food, including transport and packaging. Those costs affect what a sale is worth to a producer.

The amount on a contract is consequently different from the income a farmer keeps.

To understand that income, we would need the association's distribution of payments and the costs of transport, administration and lost or rejected products. We would also need to know whether smaller farms can take part on workable terms.

A municipal report of 2 February 2026 adds detail about transport. It says CAFC received a box truck and APRODER a dump truck. CAFC president Davi Dutra described dependence on outside transport firms. APRODER representative Thiago Degasperi expected its vehicle to carry soil-preparation inputs and reduce costs. These are attributed accounts in the municipality's report, not newsroom interviews or measured savings. municipal report dated 2 February 2026

Research elsewhere in Espírito Santo helps identify what to ask. A 2025 study across 20 municipalities found purchasing and sales difficulties, problems with public calls and limited technical capacity. Qualitative research in the state also describes how associations can open markets while facing demanding logistics, planning and administration.

Those findings guide the inquiry. Establishing whether the same difficulties affected Cariacica in 2026 would require local evidence.

What reached the school kitchen?

At the school end, the municipality reported in 2025 that its network served more than 50,000 pupils. It described weekly family-farm deliveries of vegetables, fruit, breads and other foods for menus planned by nutritionists.

That account supplies background, rather than a review of 2026 deliveries. The payment records cannot show quantities and condition on arrival, how much food was used, whether menus matched seasonal supply or what changed in children's nutrition.

Transparency does not make a programme successful

This reporting began in Albertina, Minas Gerais, where three named contracts showed the 2026 purchasing mechanism. The accessible records there allowed less detailed tracking of supplier-level execution.

That is a difference in what we could observe, rather than evidence that Albertina performed worse.

Cariacica's linked records make more of the process open to scrutiny. A resident can trace a supplier through commitments, accounting checks and payments, then ask for evidence where the record ends.

Leaving such a trail is itself a useful public capability. It helps people see what has happened and makes explanations of delay or progress easier to test.

The strongest case for the model

The model's appeal lies in connecting demand that already exists. Municipal schools buy food; family farms produce it. Public purchasing can give the two a more dependable relationship.

If the arrangements work, farms may gain the confidence to plan and invest while schools receive fresh food. The conditions of that relationship determine whether the potential becomes useful in practice.

Late calls, unsuitable seasonal requirements, costly transport and slow payment can erode that predictability. Farms with little working capital may feel delays particularly sharply. Administrative work can also fall on organisations least equipped to absorb it.

Cariacica's records show contracts, processed invoices and payments to associations. Following the people on either side would tell us whether those transactions create a dependable market and a dependable food supply.

Where the evidence stops

Documented
Cariacica has a current 2026 public call for family-farm food for PNAE, multiple named rural associations and cooperatives, signed contracts and several budget lines that have moved into liquidation and payment.
Documented
One CAFC ES line in the 3 September portal snapshot had been committed but had not yet moved into liquidation or payment.
Not established
The public records do not establish net income effects for individual farmers, distribution within associations, full delivery quality or timing, school-side outcomes, nutritional effects or ecological benefits.
Our assessment
The records let us examine how purchases and payments are carried out. They do not yet show what those transactions mean for farmers’ incomes or children’s meals.

What we will watch next

Presentation revised 18 September 2026 · Two compressed relationship labels were rewritten as reader-facing questions; the evidence and claims are unchanged.

  1. How does money move from the association to the farmer? What costs are deducted, and how quickly does it reach producers?
  2. What happens between delivery and acceptance? Which products arrive, how often, and how are changes in quantity, quality or seasonality handled?
  3. Different supplier trajectories. Why do some budget lines move quickly into payment while others remain earlier in the chain?
  4. Smaller producers. Can farms with less administrative and logistical capacity participate without being pushed behind larger organisations?
  5. School-side value. Do local purchases improve menu quality, reduce waste or increase resilience — and how would that be measured?
  6. Ecological claims. Local or family-farm purchasing should not be treated as environmental benefit without evidence about production, transport and land use.

Following the money to farmers and the food to children

Responsibility passes through several hands: the municipality orders, producers and associations organise supply, finance staff check and pay claims, and school kitchens prepare meals.

Each part needs the others to work.

The records make some of those connections visible. The next account should follow the money into the association and the food into the school, keeping the farmers' and children's experiences alongside the municipal figures.

A paid invoice is a meaningful step. What a farmer can keep from the sale, and what a child receives at lunchtime, give that step its wider purpose.
