How we work

Evidence before certainty · constructive without becoming promotional

Our editorial direction

What helps a society keep working?

We follow public ideas into everyday life: a family finding care, a farmer changing how land is used, a community keeping its water supply working. What made the change possible, who did the work and what did it cost?

By societal capacity, we mean the ability to sustain good lives, secure families, capable communities and institutions, and the natural conditions they depend on. These values guide our work without tying it to a political party.

What we look for

Start with what changes

We look for a concrete change and the people responsible for making it happen. A new rule or budget can begin the story. Its later chapters concern access, use, benefits, costs and what remains unresolved.

Useful experience may come from a public office, business, family or voluntary group. We examine how the work is done and whether it can last.

What constructive means

A promising idea gets harder questions

A promising response deserves careful questions. How does it work for someone who uses it? Who benefits or is left out? What evidence supports it, and what would change our assessment?

An initiative can be worth following before its results are known. We give that uncertainty a clear place in the story.

How we distinguish claims

A decision is not the same as a result

An allocation, a changed law and an operating service are different kinds of progress. We follow the connections between them, asking what reached the people or places the policy was intended to help.

Readers should be able to distinguish our observations, a source's evidence or claims, our interpretation and what remains unknown.

People and places

The people doing the work are part of the evidence

Someone must answer an application, negotiate over land, care for a child or repair equipment. Those practical responsibilities help explain why a policy works in one place and struggles in another.

People's experience has value in its own right. We say when we have not spoken to those affected and leave their views open rather than supplying opinions for them.

Writing and form

Let the reader enter the material without inventing the room

Clear explanation and vivid writing can work together. We use rhythm, sequence, reconstruction and artistic form where they help readers understand the material.

We keep the basis of that writing visible. Scenes, quotations, feelings and causes must have support; imagination belongs in an identified artistic context, not in an invented account of reported events.

Time and uncertainty

Some stories are worth following before the ending exists

Different changes take different amounts of time. A wetland needs seasons. A family may wait between approval and an appointment. A financed data centre may still need electricity and customers.

We follow that pace without manufacturing suspense. Waiting and missing evidence remain part of the account when they matter to understanding it.

A useful test

Six questions we return to

  1. What is actually happening?
  2. How do we know?
  3. How does the response work in practice?
  4. Who benefits, and who may be left out or harmed?
  5. What does it require in time, money, competence and natural resources?
  6. What would we need to observe next before drawing a stronger conclusion?

The experiment

Humans and AI can work together. Responsibility cannot disappear between them.

Experimental Newsroom combines journalism and artistic exploration through human–AI collaboration. We identify material AI involvement and keep editorial decisions, verification, possible harm and corrections tied to explicit responsibility. Artistic work can offer a new way to see a subject; factual reporting still needs evidence.
