How we work

Evidence before certainty · constructive without becoming promotional

Our editorial direction

We investigate what builds the capacity of a society.

That includes good lives, secure families, capable institutions, working communities and a natural foundation that can last. We are value-bound, but not party-bound.

What we look for

Effects, not symbols

We examine what actually improves people’s lives, institutional capacity and ecological conditions. Useful answers can come from public services, businesses, families, voluntary organisations and local communities. We ask what works, for whom, at what cost, and whether it can endure.

What constructive means

Not public relations

Constructive journalism is not positive coverage by default. When a proposal looks promising, we examine the response, how it works, the available evidence, what has been learned, and the limits, costs or people left outside it.

How we distinguish claims

Readers should be able to see the boundary.

We separate what we observed, what a source documents, what we infer, and what remains unknown. Official records can establish intentions, rules, funding or implementation; they do not by themselves prove that a policy worked.

People and places

Local knowledge is evidence too.

We begin where possible with people, places and the work already being done. People affected by a response are not passive scenery: their experience can reveal how the mechanism works, where it fails and what official measures overlook.

Writing and form

Bring the reader closer to the object.

We use scene, sequence, rhythm, reconstruction and artistic form when they improve understanding. The closer a passage makes a real event feel, the more clearly we must be able to trace the details that carry factual weight.

Time and uncertainty

Not every story has to know the ending.

What happened, when we discovered it and when the reader learns it are not always the same. We may follow events or discovery, but we do not manufacture mystery. A real interval — waiting, ecological time, silence or missing evidence — can remain part of the story.

A useful test

Six questions we return to

  1. What is actually happening?
  2. How do we know?
  3. How does the response work in practice?
  4. Who benefits, and who may be left out or harmed?
  5. What does it require in time, money, competence and natural resources?
  6. What would we need to observe next before drawing a stronger conclusion?

The experiment

A human–AI newsroom with explicit responsibility

Experimental Newsroom is an artistic and journalistic experiment. We may use form, scene and language to help a reader enter documented material, but we do not present invented detail as witness observation. AI contributions are identified when material to the published work, and editorial responsibility remains explicit.
