The AI boom can be real. Its financing can still be fragile

A machine in a data centre can save time at work while the debt, valuations and purchase commitments surrounding it build risk elsewhere. A new report from the Bank for International Settlements shows why the promise and the vulnerability can exist at the same time.

Why this matters

  • The BIS says AI investment supported growth and financial markets in 2025, while warning that high valuations, debt and poorly disclosed financing could amplify a reversal.
  • When an investor is also a supplier, capital can return as revenue through purchase commitments. The arrangement may be legitimate, but outside readers may struggle to see how independent the demand is.
  • This is not a forecast of an imminent crash. The public-interest test is whether investors, lenders, auditors and regulators can trace who carries the risk if expected returns arrive late.

This edition is based on public documents available on 3 September 2026. It distinguishes the BIS assessment from our analysis and does not allege wrongdoing by any named company. No company, lender, worker, regulator or affected community was interviewed for this edition.

One deal can tell several stories

An investment in an artificial-intelligence company can appear as fresh capital for the company receiving it. If that company then promises to buy chips or computing capacity from the investor, the same arrangement can also appear as future revenue for the investor. If a third company builds the data centre and leases it back under a long contract, another balance sheet and another layer of financing enter the picture.

Each transaction may be legitimate. Together they raise a simple question that is harder to answer than it sounds: How much of the AI boom is independent customer demand, and how much depends on participants financing one another's growth?

The Bank for International Settlements, an institution owned by central banks, placed that question inside its Annual Economic Report 2026. The report says optimism about AI supported investment, stock values and favourable financial conditions during 2025. It also warns that intense competition, high valuations, debt, private credit and poorly disclosed financing arrangements could amplify a reversal if the economic returns from AI arrive more slowly than investors expect.

That is not a forecast of collapse. It is a description of a transmission mechanism: how disappointment in one celebrated sector could travel into credit conditions, investment and employment elsewhere.

The boom begins with a plausible promise

The easiest version of this story is that investors have become irrational. The evidence does not justify that shortcut.

AI systems can perform some tasks faster, help people search large bodies of information and automate parts of software, administration and analysis. BIS cites task-level studies that often find time savings between 20 and 50 per cent. The same report says estimates of aggregate productivity growth tend to remain below one per cent over a long horizon because organisations must redesign work, integrate systems and solve the slowest parts of production before local gains become economy-wide gains.

The distinction matters. A technology can be useful without producing returns as quickly or as evenly as financial markets expect. A hospital may save time in one administrative process while spending more on verification, integration and security. A small company may use an AI service productively without generating enough new demand to justify the infrastructure built for millions of future users.

The uncertainty is therefore not only whether AI works. It is whether useful capabilities become reliable revenue, productivity and cash flow on the timetable embedded in today's investments.

How optimism moves beyond technology companies

The money starts moving long before an end customer pays for computing. Companies order chips, build data centres and sign long electricity contracts; equipment makers, builders, utilities and consultants receive income along the way. Rising technology shares can also increase household and institutional wealth, while easier financing encourages more borrowing and investment.

That is how expectations about tomorrow can create spending today. The same route can run backwards. If revenue disappoints, a company may cut capital spending; suppliers lose orders, lenders reassess debt, and credit can become more expensive for businesses that were never part of the original AI story.

BIS does not say this sequence must happen. It argues that high valuations, investors being paid less for taking risk, and financing that outsiders cannot easily see make the route plausible enough to examine.

The circular-financing question

The most important phrase in the BIS account is not “artificial intelligence”. It is “circular financing”.

In the pattern described by BIS, a chip maker or large cloud provider takes an equity stake in an AI laboratory or specialised cloud company. The recipient, in turn, commits to buying chips or computing capacity from the investor. Capital returns to the investor as revenue, while both parties can point to growth: one has raised money and secured capacity; the other has booked or announced future demand.

Reciprocal commercial relationships are not automatically deceptive. Long-term commitments can help finance costly infrastructure and give both sides greater certainty. The problem begins when an outside reader cannot tell how much risk has been transferred, how conditional the commitments are, whether the same assets support several obligations, or how much reported demand would remain without the financing relationship.

The useful journalistic question is therefore not, “Is circular financing bad?” It is, “What would this demand look like if the financing link were removed?”

A data centre is also a physical promise

Financial projections eventually meet land, equipment, electricity and time. The International Energy Agency's Energy and AI report stresses that AI depends on electricity for data centres and that policy makers have lacked comprehensive data for assessing the relationship. A financed project can still wait for a grid connection, equipment, permits or customers.

These physical constraints cut in two directions. Scarcity may protect the value of completed infrastructure. It may also delay revenue, raise costs or leave a carefully financed facility unable to operate as planned. A balance sheet can recognise a commitment before a power system can serve it.

The financial story is therefore inseparable from an implementation story. How many announced facilities are financed? How many are connected? How many are operating? How much of their capacity is paid for by independent, recurring use?

Where the risk could travel

BIS points to the growing role of private credit in financing AI and information-technology activity. Private credit can fund projects that banks or public markets would not finance in the same way. It can also make the full chain of exposure harder for the public to see.

If an AI-related asset is repriced, the first loss may sit with an equity investor. The next question is whether lenders, private funds, insurers or banks share exposure through loans, guarantees, leases or fund investments. The Financial Stability Board has separately warned that AI adoption in finance can amplify vulnerabilities through concentrated providers, correlated behaviour, cyber risk and model or data failures. That is a different mechanism, but it points to the same supervisory need: authorities require better information about dependencies before stress reveals them.

The public consequence would not be that every unsuccessful data centre becomes a financial crisis. It would be that a sufficiently large and connected repricing could tighten credit for smaller companies, delay investment and affect jobs far beyond the firms that made the original AI bets.

The strongest objection

Previous technology booms have sometimes built useful infrastructure even when investors lost money. Railways, fibre networks and other systems did not become worthless simply because financing expectations outran early returns. AI infrastructure may likewise support valuable services for many years.

This objection does not cancel the financial question. Social usefulness and investor return are different tests. A data centre may remain productive after its first owner fails. A technology may transform work while some loans financing its expansion still default. The public can inherit useful infrastructure and a costly financial correction at the same time.

The fair conclusion is therefore neither “the boom is safe because AI is useful” nor “AI is useless because financing may be fragile”. It is that technological value does not remove the need to see the financing clearly.

What we will watch next

  1. Capital expenditure compared with realised AI revenue and cash flow. Announced spending should be separated from completed investment and from revenue earned from independent customers.
  2. Reciprocal commitments. Companies should disclose when an investor is also a major supplier and when investment is connected to future purchase obligations.
  3. Debt, leases and private-credit exposure. Long-term contracts, guarantees and asset pledges can carry risk even when they do not look like ordinary bank loans.
  4. Physical completion and use. A financed or announced data centre is not the same as a connected, operating and substantially used facility.
  5. Productivity beyond selected tasks. Time saved in a demonstration should be separated from durable gains across an organisation or economy, including verification and integration costs.
  6. Who absorbs a reversal. Regulators should be able to trace whether losses would remain with sophisticated investors or travel through funds, insurers, banks, workers, suppliers and local communities.

What the case means — and who still has to answer

The AI boom has already become more than a technology story. It is an infrastructure story, an electricity story, a credit story and a disclosure story. The promise may be substantial. That makes financial clarity more important, not less.

AI companies still have to show how customer use becomes durable revenue. Chip makers and cloud providers have to show how much demand is independent of their own investments. Data-centre developers have to show which projects are financed, connected and occupied. Lenders and private-credit funds have to show where losses would sit. Auditors have to test whether commitments and related-party relationships are represented clearly. Regulators have to decide whether existing disclosures let them follow risk across the full chain.

Until those answers are visible, the most honest description is not that the AI boom is a bubble or that it is secure. It is that a real technological expansion is being financed through arrangements whose strength cannot yet be judged from the headline investment totals alone.
