Taiwan is inviting communities to help design the transition

Taiwan is asking 20 communities to plan practical routes to net zero, funding work on circular materials, and inviting researchers to examine how new technology changes daily life. These are calls for proposals, not proof of change — but they show what the state is asking people and institutions to build together.

Originator, editorial direction & final publication responsibility: Bjørn Moe Aldema · Writing: Verana Falk, AI investigative journalist · Editorial source and method review: Wolfgang, Illana Stein and Albert Lindberg · Published by Experimental Newsroom, 31 August 2026 · Updated 3 September 2026

This is document reporting. It analyses official call texts published by Taiwanese authorities, not the results of completed projects. Dates below are Taiwan time unless stated otherwise.

No single Taiwanese programme ties these efforts together. The pattern appears only when the calls are placed side by side: one asks communities and technical partners to plan local net-zero routes; another follows materials from packaging to buildings; a third asks how new technology may change daily interaction and human behaviour.

The clearest example is a pilot scheme from the Climate Change Administration. It invited 20 communities to develop one practical route to net zero — ranging from resilient local energy and verified carbon reduction to circular local economies and low-carbon behaviour. The local applicant must work with a technical partner. The state is therefore not merely buying equipment; it is asking organised local actors to define needs, assemble expertise and make a viable plan.

The documents establish that organised local participation is being invited and funded. They do not establish lasting participation, fairer access or better family life. Our narrower reading is that the state is creating room for communities, researchers and firms to shape parts of the transition together.

From material streams to daily life

A second call starts with things that have to go somewhere: biomass, plastic and packaging, textiles, electronics, buildings and energy equipment. Taiwan’s Resource Circulation Administration is seeking research and commercial development across those material streams, funding both earlier innovation and work closer to use. The opening is concrete — reduced packaging, reused materials, circular construction — but the applicants are Taiwanese universities, research units and industry, not residents acting alone.

A third call, from the National Science and Technology Council, puts a different question alongside high technology: how will new technologies change daily interaction and human behaviour? Its “Chip Innovation Humanities Cross-disciplinary Foresight Research” programme asks for future scenarios and demonstrators grounded in the social sciences, humanities, design and user behaviour. It does not call this family policy. Still, it creates an official research space in which technology is examined through lived experience rather than only performance specifications.

The practical test: who can take part?

These calls should not be romanticised. The community pilot is for registered local bodies, not for residents acting alone. The circular-materials call is for domestic research and industrial applicants. The human-centred technology programme is for researchers who meet Taiwan’s science-council rules. This model can mobilise capacity, but it can also favour places that already have organisations, technical partners and time to prepare an application.

That matters because the difficult question is not whether Taiwan can produce sophisticated technology. It is whether technology, transport, energy, education, care, work and nature can be arranged into places that are easier to live in. Families enter the story here not as a promise that one grant can change fertility, but as a test of whether public innovation actually lightens everyday pressures on children, parents and older people.

More than one kind of infrastructure

Other current calls widen the picture. The Climate Change Administration is supporting more efficient and innovative cooling in schools, public institutions, factories and agricultural operations. A separate Taiwan–Netherlands port-research call includes circular resources, digital tools, regulation, safety, public–private governance and local acceptance alongside energy technology. Neither is a family programme. Both show innovation moving out of the laboratory and into places where people work, learn, produce and depend on public systems.

That is why Taiwan may be worth watching not only for the technologies it makes, but for the public arrangements built around them. The next question is practical: can individual pilots become places where work, learning, care, production and ecological limits fit together — and who gets to take part when they do?
