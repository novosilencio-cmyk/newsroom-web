Ready to leave hospital. Still waiting for the next service

Tromsø lists 34 ordinary short-term beds at Helsehuset. Yet the municipality says it paid for an average of 21 somatic discharge-ready patients a day in 2025; the first-quarter 2026 average was 25. Its own reports show why counting beds is not enough: the next service must also fit the patient, have qualified staff and be ready at the right time.

Why this matters

  • Tromsø paid NOK 45.9 million (about €4.25 million) for discharge-ready patients in 2025 — NOK 10.3 million over budget and NOK 2.9 million more than in 2024.
  • The municipality says people waiting for long-term care occupy short-term places. That reduces the places available for new arrivals from hospital and pushes pressure back towards UNN.
  • Early 2026 figures show both persistence and movement: Tromsø reported an average of 25 somatic discharge-ready patients a day in the first quarter, while later regional figures and the municipality’s preliminary April assessment indicate improvement. They do not establish which measure caused it.

This document-led edition uses municipal, hospital, regional-health, supervisory, national-audit and research sources available on 4 September 2026. No patient, relative, municipal worker or hospital employee was interviewed. The article does not treat every discharge-ready patient as elderly, assume that every patient needed a Helsehuset bed, or claim that a faster transfer is automatically a safer outcome. Euro equivalents are approximate reader aids, rounded from the European Central Bank reference rate for 3 September 2026: €1 = NOK 10.8063.

Medically ready is not the end of the journey

A hospital doctor can decide that a patient no longer needs specialist treatment. That decision does not make the next service appear.

Someone may be able to return home with help. Another person may need rehabilitation, a short-term stay, round-the-clock care, adapted housing, mental-health support or a long-term nursing-home place. The municipality must assess that need and make the right service available. Until the handoff is possible, a medically ready patient can remain in hospital.

In Tromsø, the pressure is visible in both service and financial records. The municipality’s first 2026 interim report says it paid for an average of 21 somatic discharge-ready patients a day in 2025. Its 2026 budget assumed eight. The average in the first quarter was 25.

Those numbers do not describe 25 people waiting for the same thing. They describe a queue produced by several possible next services — and by the time required to turn a decision into safe, staffed care.

One discharge decision, six separate tests

  1. Medical readiness. UNN decides that specialist inpatient treatment is no longer required.
  2. Municipal assessment. Tromsø determines what support the person needs after hospital.
  3. Service match. The next step may be home care, rehabilitation, short-term care, long-term care, adapted housing or another service.
  4. Usable capacity. A room or home-care plan must have the right equipment, competence and staffing for that person.
  5. Transfer. Information, medication, timing and transport must travel with the patient.
  6. Outcome. A completed transfer still has to be safe, stable and appropriate after the hospital bed is released.

Thirty-four beds are not thirty-four exits

Tromsø describes Helsehuset as a time-limited service before, after or instead of hospital admission. It gathers the municipality’s short-term and acute places in one building. The two ordinary short-term wards, K1 and K2, have 17 beds each.

The public count is useful. It is not a daily capacity measure.

A bed may be physically present but unavailable because qualified staff are missing. It may be occupied by someone whose short-term stay has become a wait for long-term care. It may not suit a person’s clinical or behavioural needs. And a patient who could return home may need a care package that cannot begin on the required day.

Tromsø’s own 2025 annual report describes this downstream queue directly. Long stays in long-term nursing-home places reduce turnover. Short-term places are then used by people waiting for long-term placement. Fewer short-term places remain available to receive people from hospital, the number of discharge-ready patients rises, and staffing pressure increases.

This is a capacity-conversion problem. The question is not only how many beds exist. It is how many suitable services can be staffed and opened at the moment each person needs one.

The queue has a price, but the price is not the whole cost

Norwegian municipalities pay hospitals for qualifying days after a patient is declared discharge-ready and the municipal duty to receive the patient applies. Helse Nord lists the 2026 rate as NOK 6,172 per day (about €571).

Tromsø’s annual report says the municipality paid NOK 45.9 million (about €4.25 million) for discharge-ready patients in 2025. That was NOK 10.3 million over budget and NOK 2.9 million more than in 2024.

The payment is a clear public cost. It should not be confused with a complete welfare calculation. A hospital bed used after specialist treatment is finished can constrain other hospital activity. Opening municipal capacity can require staff, premises, transport and equipment. Sending someone home too quickly can create new risk for the patient, relatives and home-care workers.

The cheapest transfer on paper is not necessarily the right one. The relevant comparison is between safe alternatives across the whole chain.

The mechanism has been documented for years

The pressure did not begin in 2026. UNN’s public records show that Tromsø’s somatic overstay days rose from 3,736 in 2019 to 8,190 in 2022. Tromsø accounted for 51 percent of UNN’s somatic overstay days in 2022.

After an inspection that year, the supervisory authority concluded that Tromsø had not ensured adequate planning and dimensioning for discharge-ready patients needing municipal services. The report identified limited nursing-home capacity, short-term places occupied by people waiting for long-term care, staffing problems and weaknesses in coordination.

The municipality responded with a broad package. It described 16 supported-care housing units at Laureng, possible use of double rooms at Helsehuset, purchases of places from other municipalities, stronger home-based services, weekly strategic coordination, possible temporary use of 24 rooms at Seminaret and longer-term building plans.

That response matters because it shows the municipality was not relying on one type of bed. It also creates an evidence test: an announced measure is not the same as funded, staffed, operating capacity.

The 2025 annual report says measures to reduce discharge-ready patients had been evaluated but had not produced the desired result. It also records that 24 nursing-home beds on the fifth floor of Seminaret were discontinued, and that Helsehuset reduced staffing and operating costs during the year. The report does not by itself establish the net capacity effect of every opening, closure or staffing change.

High spending can coexist with unavailable care

Tromsø’s 2024 knowledge base describes a service area using more resources than comparable municipalities. Its need-adjusted resource-use indicator for municipal care services was 29.4 percent above the ASSS average in 2023. For 2022, it reported high costs per recipient, 0.77 full-time equivalents per care-service recipient and NOK 200 million in overtime and agency purchases.

At the same time, the municipality reported a lower share of health-trained staff than the ASSS average. Planned staffing and service levels could not always be filled by people with the required qualifications. The result, it said, could be costly agency purchases or services not being delivered.

The 2025 annual report repeats the practical tension. High sickness absence, vacancies and recruitment problems generated overtime and agency use. At night, competence requirements could make an available ordinary substitute unsuitable; safety could require extra staffing instead.

These figures do not prove that Tromsø spends efficiently or inefficiently. They show why a budget line, a staffing plan and a room cannot be counted as three independent pieces of capacity. They must combine in the same shift.

April may mark improvement. It is not yet a verdict

The first 2026 interim report contains an important counterweight to a story of permanent failure. Preliminary April figures, the municipality said, suggested that measures to receive more patients from UNN were having a good effect.

The latest regional monthly figures we found in our 4 September review were for May. Helse Nord reported that UNN averaged 60 discharge-ready patients a day in January, including 41 from Tromsø. By May, those averages had fallen to 48 and 36.

But these are not one seamless series. Tromsø’s interim figure is a somatic daily average. Helse Nord’s January and May totals include somatic care, mental-health care and substance-use treatment. The Directorate of Health has also recalculated national somatic data after changes in reporting and method.

The direction is encouraging. The cause remains open. Season, hospital activity, patient mix, temporary capacity and several municipal measures may all contribute. A preliminary source assessment should not be converted into an independent causal finding.

The municipality’s own 2026 forecast reflects that uncertainty. It expected discharge-ready patients to create a NOK 17 million overspend for the year while assuming the April improvement would continue: about 15 somatic patients a day through August and eight thereafter.

A local chain inside a larger system

Tromsø should not be treated as if it alone created pressure at UNN. The city hosts the hospital, distance affects how people use services, and a national audit of hospital use among people over 80 found disease burden to be the strongest measured explanation of variation between municipalities.

Still, the regional contrast is large. Under the Directorate of Health’s recalculated method, the UNN catchment area recorded 53.5 somatic overstay days per 1,000 residents in 2025, compared with 13.6 nationally. Average overstay was 8.6 days in the UNN area and 3.5 days nationwide.

That comparison establishes a regional problem, not a Tromsø cause. It is useful because it prevents the January queue from being dismissed as a single-day anecdote while keeping the geography honest.

Where the evidence stops

Documented
Tromsø publicly lists 34 ordinary short-term beds at Helsehuset; its reports describe a queue from long-term care into short-term capacity and from there back towards hospital.
Documented
The municipality paid NOK 45.9 million for discharge-ready patients in 2025 and reported an average of 25 somatic discharge-ready patients a day in the first quarter of 2026.
Source assessment
Tromsø’s interim report says preliminary April figures indicated that measures to receive more patients had a good effect. The published material does not isolate the contribution of each measure.
Not established
The accessible sources do not provide a month-by-month count of staffed and patient-appropriate Helsehuset places, or show which next service every waiting patient needed.
Not established
The 2022 supervisory page does not establish whether the legal non-compliance is formally closed in September 2026. This article describes the dated finding, not a current legal status.
Not established
The documents do not measure patient experience, safety after transfer, readmission, unpaid family care or the full cost of alternative municipal capacity.
Our assessment
The evidence supports an implementation story about capacity conversion. It does not support a one-cause verdict about “too few beds”.

What should be counted next

  1. Need, not only headcount. Publish how many waiting patients need home care, short-term care, long-term care, adapted housing, mental-health or substance-use services.
  2. Staffed capacity. Separate approved rooms, available beds, staffed beds, beds suited to the patient and places actually offered each day.
  3. Time at every handoff. Measure the interval from medical readiness to municipal assessment, service decision, available place and completed transfer.
  4. Implementation dates. For each announced measure, show when it was funded, staffed, opened, reduced or closed — and for how many people.
  5. Safety after transfer. Track readmission, adverse events, continuity, patient experience and pressure on relatives as well as hospital days released.
  6. Comparable methods. Keep somatic and broader service totals separate, and do not join older hospital-system counts to recalculated national data without a documented bridge.
  7. Whole-chain cost. Compare hospital payments with the cost and quality of realistic municipal alternatives, including workforce and agency dependence.

Count the service that is ready, not only the bed that exists

Medical readiness for discharge is a clinical decision. The municipal service that follows is an operating system.

Tromsø’s documents show the difference. The city has beds, buildings, plans and substantial care spending. It also has long-term queues occupying short-term places, difficulty filling shifts with qualified people and a hospital bill that exceeded its 2025 budget.

The early 2026 improvement deserves to be followed, not discounted. Its real test is whether it lasts and whether people move into services that are safe and suitable — not merely whether a hospital count falls.

A public capacity promise becomes real only when the right service, the right competence and the right timing meet around one person.
