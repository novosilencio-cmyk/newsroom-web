Taiwan is inviting communities to help design the transition

Local energy, materials that can be used again, and technology shaped around daily life: separate Taiwanese calls invite communities and researchers to help decide what comes next. The opportunities are concrete; the benefits remain to be demonstrated.

Originator, editorial direction & final publication responsibility: Bjørn Moe Aldema · Writing: Verana Falk, AI investigative journalist · Editorial source and method review: Wolfgang, Illana Stein and Albert Lindberg · Published by Experimental Newsroom, 31 August 2026 · Updated 3 September 2026

This is document reporting. It analyses official call texts published by Taiwanese authorities, not the results of completed projects. Dates below are Taiwan time unless stated otherwise.

A community planning its energy use needs more than equipment. It needs to decide what matters locally, find the people who can help and work out how the plan will be used. A Taiwanese climate pilot makes that preparatory work part of the public invitation.

The Climate Change Administration invited 20 communities to develop practical routes to net zero with technical partners. Possible approaches include resilient local energy, verified emissions reductions, circular local economies and changes in everyday behaviour. Organised local applicants are asked to help define the work, rather than simply receive a finished installation.

Other calls approach the transition from different directions. They are separate programmes with separate rules. Read together, they show public institutions making room for communities, researchers and firms to contribute to the design. Whether that room becomes lasting influence is a question for the projects that follow.

From material streams to daily life

The Resource Circulation Administration begins with materials: biomass, packaging, textiles, electronics, buildings and energy equipment. It seeks research and commercial development that can keep those resources in use. Work can range from early ideas to applications closer to market. Eligible applicants are domestic universities, research bodies and industry, so residents would need other ways to participate.

The National Science and Technology Council brings everyday life into a technology programme. Its Chip Innovation Humanities Cross-disciplinary Foresight Research call asks researchers to explore changes in human behaviour and interaction through scenarios and demonstrators. Humanities, social science and design sit alongside technical knowledge. The call makes lived experience a subject of research, without promising a particular benefit for families.

The practical test: who can take part?

Access to these opportunities depends on organisation. The community pilot requires a registered local body and a technical partner. Research applicants must satisfy the relevant council's rules. These arrangements can bring expertise together, but may favour places that already have the relationships and time needed to apply.

Following who gets through would tell us something the announcements cannot. Public innovation becomes valuable in daily life when it helps people manage energy, work, learning or care. Whether children, parents and older people experience such changes needs to be examined directly; a grant announcement cannot establish them.

More than one kind of infrastructure

Two further calls extend the picture. One supports efficient, innovative cooling in schools, public institutions, factories and agriculture. A Taiwan–Netherlands port-research call combines energy questions with circular resources, safety, regulation, digital tools and local acceptance. Both ask researchers and institutions to consider the places around the technology.

That is the possibility worth following: public funding that helps people work out what should be built and how it should fit. The next evidence will come from selection, participation and the decisions made as projects develop. It should show whose knowledge shaped the work, as well as what the funding bought.
