A contract is not a school lunch. Cariacica lets us follow what happens next

In Cariacica, Espírito Santo, municipal records connect Brazil’s 2026 family-farm school-food procurement to contracts, invoices, liquidation and payment. The trail is unusually visible — and it shows exactly where the evidence still stops.

Why this matters

  • Cariacica’s Contract 042/2026 with the rural producers’ association APRODER is worth about R$1.24 million (roughly €209,000) and covers a long list of foods for municipal school meals.
  • Municipal records show money moving beyond the signature: one APRODER budget line was fully liquidated and paid at R$277,817.65 (about €46,800), while APROVISTA had R$182,981.08 (about €30,900) liquidated and paid on another line.
  • The same public call also contains lines at earlier stages. One CAFC ES budget line showed R$35,815.58 (about €6,000) committed but nothing yet liquidated or paid in the 3 September snapshot. The record does not explain why.

This document-led edition uses Cariacica municipal transparency records, current FNDE guidance and independent research from Espírito Santo checked on 3 September 2026. No farmer, association member, school worker or municipal official was interviewed for this edition. Euro equivalents are approximate reader aids, rounded from the 3 September 2026 BRL/EUR rate of 1 real ≈ €0.1686. A payment to an association is not treated as proof of individual farmer income, nutritional outcome or ecological benefit.

The food is visible before the outcome

A public food programme can look complete long before anybody has eaten lunch.

A rule creates a purchasing route. A city publishes a call. An association signs a contract. A budget line appears. Each step matters. None of them, by itself, tells us whether the food arrived at the right school, whether the supplier was paid in time, or whether a farmer ended the season with more room to invest.

Cariacica gives us something more useful than another announcement. Its 2026 records let us follow several handoffs in the same procurement chain.

Contract 042/2026 was signed with Associação dos Produtores Rurais de Roda D’Água e Região — APRODER — on 3 March. The contracted value is R$1,238,598.55 (about €209,000). The item table is physical rather than abstract: mozzarella, lettuce, plantain — a firm, starchy cooking banana — beetroot, scallions, carrots, chayote, kale, cauliflower, yam, oranges, fruit pulp, yoghurt, butter, cornmeal, organic bananas, zucchini and cassava — a starchy root — are among the listed foods.

Chayote may be the least familiar item on that list to many readers. It is a light-green, often pear-shaped relative of squash and melon. Botanically it is a fruit, but it is normally cooked and eaten as a vegetable. Its flesh is crisp and very mild, with a faintly sweet, cucumber-like taste.

The contract is split across several budget commitments. That matters because a single supplier relationship can appear in different school categories or funding lines. A grand total built by simply adding every visible number would risk double-counting or mixing stages. The safer question is smaller: how far did each line actually move?

Follow the money further than the signature

Brazilian public accounts expose several stages after a contract is signed. An empenho commits budget money to an obligation. A liquidação is the stage where the municipality recognises the amount due after checking the claim and supporting documents. Payment comes after that.

For one APRODER line under Contract 042/2026, Cariacica’s 3 September portal snapshot showed R$277,817.65 (about €46,800) committed, fully liquidated and fully paid. A separate payment record dated 31 August shows R$102,285.10 (about €17,200) paid to APRODER under the same 2026 family-farm school-meal call; the linked liquidation identifies invoice NF 000.000.606.

Another association, APROVISTA — Associação de Produtores Rurais de Boa Vista — appears under Contract 041/2026. One budget line records R$196,072.26 (about €33,100) committed, R$182,981.08 (about €30,900) liquidated and the same R$182,981.08 paid. The corresponding payment was made on 13 July, three days after the liquidation, which cites invoice 000.000.199.

What the public record can show

  1. Contract. A named association has a legal agreement to supply food.
  2. Budget commitment. The municipality has assigned money to that obligation.
  3. Liquidation. The claim has passed an accounting check and an amount due has been recognised.
  4. Payment. Municipal money has actually been transferred to the named organisation.
  5. Still open. The public ledger does not by itself tell us what each member farmer earned, how costly delivery was, how the food performed in school kitchens or what changed for children.

The sequence shows that the procurement did not stop at a signature page. It also gives us a clean place to stop making claims.

Payment is evidence of execution. It is not evidence of outcome.

The same public call can be in several places at once

The most useful feature of Cariacica’s portal may be that it does not flatten the programme into one success number.

In the 3 September snapshot, APROVISTA had most of one committed line already liquidated and paid. A line for ASCOPRUM — Associação Comunitária de Produtores Rurais de Munguba — showed R$77,479.13 (about €13,100) committed and R$58,069.46 (about €9,800) liquidated and paid. On the same municipal screen, one line for Cooperativa da Agricultura Familiar de Cariacica, CAFC ES, showed R$35,815.58 (about €6,000) committed and R$0 liquidated or paid.

That last figure is not evidence of failure. There may be timing, delivery, documentation, scheduling or other explanations that the line itself does not provide. The point is narrower: the same policy, municipality and public call can contain suppliers at different execution stages.

That is exactly the kind of difference a serious implementation watch should preserve rather than average away.

The missing farmer inside the paid association

The public record becomes thin again once the municipality pays an organisation.

FNDE’s current pricing guidance recognises that food does not teleport from a farm to a school. Acquisition prices are supposed to account for local supply conditions and for costs required by the public call, including freight, packaging and other expenses needed to deliver the food.

That makes one distinction unavoidable: gross contract value ≠ net farmer income.

A payment to an association cannot tell us, from the ledger alone, how the money was distributed among members, what transport cost, whether products were lost or rejected, how much administrative work the organisation carried, or whether a smaller producer could participate on the same terms as a larger one.

This is not a theoretical concern invented for Cariacica. A 2025 study involving 20 municipalities in Espírito Santo identified operational purchase-and-sale problems, difficulties connected to public calls, reduced technical capacity and other barriers to good PNAE execution. Earlier qualitative research in the state has likewise shown that associations and cooperatives can open markets and reduce administrative burdens while also creating logistical, planning and organisational challenges.

Those studies do not prove that Cariacica has the same problems in 2026. They tell us which questions deserve to follow the money.

A paid invoice still has to become food in a school

Cariacica’s own school-food reporting gives this accounting trail a physical destination. In 2025, the municipality said its network served more than 50,000 pupils and that family-farm products were delivered weekly to municipal schools. The city described fresh vegetables, fruit, breads and other items moving into menus planned by nutritionists.

That is useful context, not a 2026 outcome audit. The payment records we examined do not tell us whether every 2026 item arrived in the right quantity and condition, how much was used or wasted, whether menu planning matched seasonal supply, or whether pupils experienced any nutritional improvement.

The state can therefore complete one handoff while the larger public purpose remains open.

Transparency does not make a programme successful

We began this reporting line in Albertina, Minas Gerais. There, three named 2026 contracts made the family-farm mechanism concrete. But the public surfaces we could reach did not let us follow supplier-level execution nearly as far.

That does not mean Albertina performed worse. It means the public chain was harder to observe.

Cariacica changes the reporting question because a citizen can move from supplier and contract to budget line, liquidation and payment. That transparency does not prove success. It makes success and failure more testable.

There is a broader lesson here. Public capacity is not only the ability to spend money or create a rule. It also includes the ability to leave a reliable enough trail that people can see whether one stage became the next.

The strongest case for the model

The constructive argument for family-farm purchasing in school meals is simple. Schools already need food. Family farms already need markets. Public purchasing can connect those two needs without inventing a separate demand system.

If the mechanism works, existing public spending can create more predictable demand for local producers while putting fresh food into schools. That is a good reason to investigate the model. It is also a good reason to test it harder.

A market is not predictable if calls are late, requested products do not fit production seasons, paperwork blocks smaller suppliers, freight consumes the margin, or payment arrives too slowly for farms with little working capital. The same policy can build capacity or shift administrative and financial burden downstream.

Cariacica gives us evidence that parts of the conversion chain are functioning: contracts exist, invoices are entering the accounting system and money is reaching named organisations. The next question is whether that institutional movement becomes durable value for the people and schools at either end.

Where the evidence stops

Documented
Cariacica has a current 2026 public call for family-farm food for PNAE, multiple named rural associations and cooperatives, signed contracts and several budget lines that have moved into liquidation and payment.
Documented
One CAFC ES line in the 3 September portal snapshot had been committed but had not yet moved into liquidation or payment.
Not established
The public records do not establish net income effects for individual farmers, distribution within associations, full delivery quality or timing, school-side outcomes, nutritional effects or ecological benefits.
Our assessment
The mechanism is mature for an implementation story about visible handoffs. It is not mature for an outcome verdict.

What we will watch next

  1. Association → farmer. How are payments distributed, what costs are deducted, and how quickly does money reach producers?
  2. Delivery → acceptance. Which products arrive, how often, and what happens when quantity, quality or seasonality changes?
  3. Different supplier trajectories. Why do some budget lines move quickly into payment while others remain earlier in the chain?
  4. Smaller producers. Can farms with less administrative and logistical capacity participate without being pushed behind larger organisations?
  5. School-side value. Do local purchases improve menu quality, reduce waste or increase resilience — and how would that be measured?
  6. Ecological claims. Local or family-farm purchasing should not be treated as environmental benefit without evidence about production, transport and land use.

The public promise should be measured at every handoff

A national programme can create the route. A municipality can turn it into contracts. An association can turn a contract into deliveries and invoices. A finance department can turn an accepted claim into payment. A school can turn food into meals.

No single actor carries the whole purpose.

Cariacica is worth watching because several of those baton passes are unusually visible. We can see that public purchasing power moved beyond intention and into real payments. We can also see the point where the ledger stops answering the most human questions.

When the association is paid, what reaches the farmer — and when the food reaches the school, what value reaches the child?

That is not a reason to withhold the story. It is the reason to keep following it.
